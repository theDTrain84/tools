Data centers on the ballot · November 4, 2026Understanding the week, from the record

Ten states · 46 local ballot questions · November 3, 2026

46 data-center questions were on local ballots in ten states on November 3. Of the 44 with a count, voters took the side that limits data centers in 43. Ohio had 21 of the questions, in 18 places, and the limiting side won all 21.

The one exception was the City of Harwood, North Dakota, where a charter amendment requiring review of large industrial projects failed, 105 to 325. [1][2] Two Maine towns, Brownfield and Freedom, had posted no count by Wednesday morning. [3] The roster is Ballotpedia’s 41 questions plus five Maine and Kansas towns added by a second tracker; the counts are the county and town sheets where this desk could reach them, and a tracker’s read where it could not. [3][4][5][6] Every count on this page is unofficial until the canvasses close.

A data center is a building full of computers that stores and serves the internet’s websites, apps and AI models, and it draws electricity and cooling water around the clock. The questions below asked a town or city whether to forbid one, cap its size, pause permits, require a public vote, keep or repeal a rezoning, or set terms on a tax break.

What the words mean

Data centers used 4.4 percent of U.S. electricity in 2023. A town votes on one because zoning, a city charter and a tax break are all local decisions.

The building, the power it draws, and the eight kinds of question that were on the ballot.

Every search, message, video, cloud file and AI answer runs on a computer somewhere, and that somewhere is a data center: a warehouse-sized building of server racks that runs day and night. The Department of Energy’s December 2024 report, prepared by Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, put U.S. data centers at about 176 terawatt-hours of electricity in 2023, 4.4 percent of everything the country used, up from 58 terawatt-hours in 2014, and projected 6.7 to 12 percent of U.S. electricity by 2028. [7] The Energy Information Administration said in January 2026 that the country was in its strongest four-year run of electricity demand growth since 2000, fueled by data centers; in May it put servers at about 7 percent of commercial-sector electricity in 2025; and its September outlook has U.S. electricity sales at 4,135 billion kilowatt-hours in 2026 and 4,211 billion in 2027, driven by data centers and manufacturing. [8][9][10]

A town votes on one because the decisions that let a data center be built are local. Where a building may go is zoning. What a city may do at all is written in its charter, which voters can amend by petition; in Ohio, the state constitution sets the signatures needed at 10 percent of the votes cast at the last general municipal election, the rule the Supreme Court of Ohio applied in the Trenton case below. [11] A council’s rezoning ordinance can be sent to the voters by petition. And a property tax abatement or a sales-tax exemption is an agreement a council or a state authority signs, which voters can condition. A megawatt is a measure of power demand; five of Ohio’s amendments draw their line at 25 megawatts, the same line the state’s utility regulator used in 2025 for its data-center tariff. [12][13][14][15][16][17]

  • Charter ban

    Adds a line to the city’s charter forbidding data centers. The charter is the city’s own constitution; a future council cannot repeal the line without another public vote.

    Urbana, Piqua, Pataskala, Granville, Upper Sandusky, Edgewater, Alhambra
  • Size cap in megawatts

    Forbids only data centers above a power line, 25 megawatts in the five Ohio amendments; smaller server rooms stay legal. A yes vote limits.

    Trenton, Defiance, Conneaut, Oregon, Sunbury; Ottawa, Kansas; Emporia’s 10 MW
  • Moratorium

    A pause on data-center permits and applications for a set time, 180 days in most of the Maine towns, while the town writes rules.

    Peru, Wells, Wiscasset, Eliot, Sabattus, Brownfield, Freedom (Maine); Bloomington’s advisory asks for one
  • Voter-approval requirement

    Large projects go to a public vote before the city may approve them. The building is still allowed; the decision moves from council to the ballot.

    Grove City (50 acres, 20 MW or 500,000 gallons a day); Janesville ($450 million on the former GM site)
  • Rezoning referendum

    Council already rezoned the land; petitioners put that ordinance to the voters. Yes keeps the rezoning, no repeals it, so here a no vote is the side that limits.

    Wilmington’s four ordinances; Ashville’s referendum on a negotiating term sheet works the same way
  • Advisory question

    Nonbinding. It tells officials what residents want and changes no law. Some asked “allow?” and some asked “limit?”, so the limiting side is no on the first and yes on the second.

    Five Illinois townships and Morgan County; Monroeville; Downe Township
  • Tax-abatement terms, or allow

    Sets conditions a developer must meet to get an incentive, or asks outright whether to add data centers as an allowed use. On an allow question, no is the side that limits.

    Perry’s financial guarantees; Boulder City’s land-use question
  • Adopt township zoning

    Two Adams County townships had no zoning at all. Adopting it gives the trustees a say over where a data center may go on farmland. A review requirement, Harwood’s question, adds an independent review and a hearing before approval.

    Monroe Township, Sprigg Township; Harwood, North Dakota

“Limiting side” is the votes cast for the side that limits data centers, divided by yes plus no. On a ban, cap, moratorium, voter-approval, review or “limit?” advisory question that side is yes; on an “allow?” advisory, an allow question, a rezoning referendum or Ashville’s agreement referendum it is no. Margin is the limiting side’s lead. Ballotpedia’s election-night table appears to have coded the no side as yes for Wilmington’s four referenda, and it ran Granville inverted; the county sheets are used here. Butler County · City of Trenton

Issue 17 passed 3,541 to 903. The Supreme Court of Ohio put it on the ballot on September 1, after council declined to certify a petition with 336 valid signatures where 128 were needed.

The charter amendment, the case, the annexation the city withdrew, the county’s moratorium and the campus already under construction.

3,541to 903

Issue 17, City of Trenton, proposed charter amendment. 79.7 percent yes, 8 of 8 precincts, unofficial. [12]

25 MW

The amendment adds a section to Article IX of the charter prohibiting construction of data centers with aggregate monthly demand or peak load above 25 megawatts. The Prologis campus already under construction is outside it. The full text was not obtained by this desk; the opinion and the ballot summary describe it. [11][12]

  1. October 2025

    141 acres sold. The City of Trenton sold 141 acres to Prologis at about $55,000 an acre, about $7.7 million, as reported by Data Center Dynamics and WYSO. [52][53]

  2. June 16, 2026

    The county moratorium. The Butler County commissioners voted unanimously for a six-month moratorium, effective immediately, on the approval, establishment, construction or permitting of any data center in the six townships under county zoning: Hanover, Lemon, Madison, Milford, Oxford and Ross. It runs to about mid-December 2026. The resolution number was not found in the county’s online records. [54][55]

  3. July 2026

    The tax abatement. Council approved a Community Reinvestment Area agreement with Prologis: a 15-year, 75 percent property tax abatement for a campus of four single-story buildings, roughly 880,000 to 1,000,000 square feet in all, with an on-site substation. [52][53]

  4. August 2026

    The annexation. Trenton petitioned to annex about 601 acres in Madison and St. Clair townships, 358 of them in St. Clair, through the expedited process for a significant economic development project, an Amazon Web Services campus. Both townships filed objections. [56][57]

  5. September 1, 2026

    The Supreme Court. In State ex rel. Blankenship v. Trenton City Council, 2026-Ohio-3406, No. 2026-1035, the court granted a limited writ of mandamus. Council’s refusal to certify the charter petition was unlawful: under Article XVIII, Section 9 of the Ohio Constitution the threshold is 10 percent of the votes cast at the last general municipal election, 1,277 votes in November 2025, so 128 signatures, and the petition carried 336 valid ones. The court ordered the amendment to the voters at a special election 60 to 120 days after the certifying ordinance. Council then certified it for November 3. [11][58]

  6. Mid-September 2026

    The annexation withdrawn. One day before the county commissioners’ public hearing, Trenton withdrew the annexation petition. The petitioners asked that any hearing be moved to a regular commissioners’ meeting on or after November 10, 2026, and reserved the right to refile. [57]

  7. November 3, 2026

    Issue 17. 3,541 yes, 903 no. [12]

  8. Open

    No new annexation petition or hearing date was on the county agenda when this desk looked. The county moratorium ends in about mid-December. A reported capacity figure for the Prologis campus appears in press coverage only; no city or county document stating it was obtained, so none is printed here. [52][54][57]

Prohibit new data center sales tax exemption agreements

The House companion to S.B. 374: no new exemption agreements after October 1, 2026.

Introduced May 27, 2026House Ways and Means Committee since June 3 [62]

The sales-tax exemption

Ohio Revised Code 122.175 lets the Ohio Tax Credit Authority grant a full or partial sales and use tax exemption on computer data center equipment, and on its delivery, installation and repair, under an agreement with the operator. [63] JobsOhio lists the exemption among the state’s incentive programs. [64] S.B. 374 and H.B. 957 would end new agreements; the Senate’s version of H.B. 646 would trim the exemption. [59][60][62]

The utility regulator’s tariff

On July 9, 2025, the Public Utilities Commission of Ohio adopted the stipulation among AEP Ohio, commission staff and the Consumers’ Counsel in Case No. 24-508-EL-ATA: new data-center customers above 25 megawatts pay for at least 85 percent of their subscribed capacity for up to 12 years, with exit fees, and AEP was ordered to file the tariffs and lift its moratorium on new data-center connections. [17][65]

November 18, Columbus

The third Ohio AI Summit meets at Columbus State Community College’s Mitchell Hall. Its 10:20 AM session, “AI and Ohio’s Innovation Infrastructure Advantage,” is described on the agenda as covering “data centers and energy systems” through “advanced manufacturing and the companies supplying critical equipment.” The listed speakers are the City of Westerville’s economic development director, Vertiv’s chief innovation officer and Google’s head of data center market development for the Midwest. [66]

What happens next

Ohio’s canvass runs November 14 to 24. Trenton’s annexation hearing, if refiled, comes on or after November 10. The county moratorium ends in about mid-December. Two Maine towns still owe a count.

  • Nov. 10

    Trenton’s annexation. The earliest date the petitioners asked for a rescheduled hearing before the Butler County commissioners. No new petition or hearing was on the county agenda when this desk looked. [57]

  • Nov. 14 to 24

    The official canvass in Ohio. Each county board begins its canvass between the eleventh and fifteenth day after the election and finishes by the twenty-first; for November 3 that is November 14 to 24. Provisional and late-arriving absentee ballots are counted then, and every Ohio number above can move. The other nine states run their own calendars. [67]

  • Nov. 18

    The Ohio AI Summit in Columbus, with its 10:20 AM infrastructure session. [66]

  • Mid-December

    Butler County’s moratorium on data centers in the six county-zoned townships reaches six months from June 16. [54][55]

  • Pending

    Brownfield and Freedom, Maine, had posted no count on their moratorium questions by Wednesday morning. [3][35][36] Emporia’s and Ottawa’s bans in Kansas wait on the district courts. [3][42][43] California’s and Nevada’s counts continue as mail ballots arrive. [44][47]
