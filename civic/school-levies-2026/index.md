# School levies 2026

> Eleven school tax questions were on ballots in Butler, Hamilton, Warren and Clermont counties on November 3, 2026. All six earned-income taxes failed; three of five property questions passed. What a mill is in dollars, what an earned-income tax is, every result on one board, Cincinnati Public's switch from an income tax to a 7-mill levy, what each district said it would cut or build, where each district's money comes from today, the state law that changed which levies are allowed, and what happens before the canvass closes November 24. ## Eleven school tax questions went to voters in four counties around Cincinnati on November 3: all six earned-income taxes failed, and three of five property questions passed, Cincinnati Public’s 7 mills, Sycamore’s 6.95 and Lakota’s $223 million bond by 1,461 votes.

Winton Woods’ 6.95-mill levy and Bethel-Tate’s renewal failed. The counts are the county boards’ election-night totals with every precinct reporting. [1][2][3][4] They stay unofficial until the boards finish the canvass, which state law places between November 14 and November 24. [46]

Earned-income taxes failed in Fairfield, North College Hill, Northwest, Southwest, Franklin and Lebanon. [1][2][3]

Property questions passed in Cincinnati, Sycamore and Lakota and failed in Winton Woods and Bethel-Tate. [1][2][4]

A school levy is a tax that voters approve or reject themselves, at the ballot box. These eleven questions asked for money in two currencies, a share of a home’s value or a share of a paycheck, and every paycheck question lost. Each district published what passage would build or what failure would cut; those lists are below in the districts’ own words.

What the words mean

### A mill is $35 a year on a $100,000 house. An earned-income tax is a share of every resident’s paycheck.

The eleven questions used two kinds of tax. The arithmetic for each is on the record.

The Ottawa County Auditor’s plain sentence: When talking about taxes, a mill is $1.00 in taxes for every $1,000 of assessed value. And the second half: In Ohio, the assessed value of real estate is 35% of the property’s estimated market value. [8] The Hamilton County Treasurer’s glossary gives the same definition. [9]

$35,000 assessed value of a $100,000 house, at 35 percent [8]

$35 one mill on that house, a year, before credits; Southwest Local’s sheet runs the same math [20]

$245 seven mills, the figure on Cincinnati Public’s own post [11]

$243 · 6.95 mills, Winton Woods’ and Sycamore’s figure [23][27]

Lakota’s 2.2-mill bond works out to $77 a year per $100,000, by the district’s page. [13] Bethel-Tate’s 5.48 mills is $192. [27] Sycamore’s treasurer wrote that the effective rate of its levy will be below 6.95 mills from the first collection year. [22]

An earned-income tax is a school district income tax on earned income only: wages, salaries and self-employment income of the people who live in the district. Cincinnati Public’s July 27 post described its proposed 0.75 percent tax as excluding Social Security, pensions, retirement and investment income, and as applying to residents only, so a person who works in the district and lives elsewhere would pay nothing. [10] Fairfield’s table put its 1.25 percent at $125 a year on $10,000 of earned income and $1,250 on $100,000. [16] Southwest Local has collected a 0.75 percent earned-income tax since January 1, 2007, and asked for a quarter point more. [21][20]

1.25% · Fairfield and North College Hill: $125 a year on $10,000 earned [16][17]

$1,250 the same tax on $100,000 of earned income [16]

7.81 mills the property levy Lebanon said would raise the same $13.5 million as its 1 percent income tax [25]

$0 what a person who works in Cincinnati and lives elsewhere would have paid under the July plan [10]

Two neighboring districts can ask in different currencies because the two taxes land on different people: a property levy on the owners of homes and businesses by value, an earned-income tax on residents by paycheck, with retirees’ pensions and Social Security left out. [10][8] Franklin and Lebanon each paired their income-tax question with a property-tax rollback if it passed, one mill in Franklin and 2.15 mills over two years in Lebanon. [24][25] Northwest’s 1.5 percent was intended to replace two property levies expiring in 2027 and 2029. [27] Cincinnati Public’s board placed one kind in July and the other in August; that record is below.

Cincinnati Public’s switch

### The board voted for a 0.75 percent earned-income tax on July 27, rescinded it on August 3 and placed 7 mills. The levy passed with 60.9 percent.

From the district’s two posts and WVXU’s account of the board votes.

- July 27, 2026 **The board approved a five-year 0.75 percent earned-income tax** for the November 3 ballot, projected to raise about $74 million a year. The district’s examples: $375 a year on $50,000 of earned income, $750 on $100,000. Social Security, pensions, retirement and investment income were excluded, and so were people who work in the district and live outside it. [10] WVXU reported the resolution narrowly passed, with board member Eve Bolton voting no. [12]
- Aug. 3, 2026 **The board rescinded the earned-income tax and placed a five-year 7-mill property levy** on the same ballot. The vote was 5 in favor, 1 opposed (Kendra Mapp) and 1 abstention (Vice President Kareem Moffett), per WVXU. [12] The district’s post says the board acted after hearing significant community concern about the impact an earned-income tax would have on working residents. [11] Between the two votes, WVXU reported, the Hamilton County Democratic Party’s executive committee voted 50 to 23 to ask the board to reconsider, and the county Republican chair also opposed the income tax. The state rule

### Since the legislature overrode the governor’s veto, a school district may no longer place replacement, emergency or substitute levies on the ballot.

What House Bill 96 changed about levies, what was vetoed, what was overridden and when, and what a district that lost on November 3 may put back on the ballot.

- June 30, 2025 **The governor signed Am. Sub. H.B. 96,** the state’s two-year operating budget, with 67 line-item vetoes. [29][30] Three of the vetoes concerned school levies. Item 66, “School District Property Tax Levy Restrictions,” pages 48 to 52 of the veto message: > This item eliminates the authority for political subdivisions to levy replacement property tax levies and the authority for school districts to levy fixed-sum emergency, substitute emergency levies, and combined school district income tax and fixed-sum property tax levies. These levies serve as important tools for school districts as they seek to maintain their long-term financial stability. … Therefore, the veto of this item is in the public interest. > > Veto message, Item 66, pp. 48 to 52 [31] Item 65, “20-Mill Floor Calculation,” page 48: This item requires that emergency and substitute tax levies, incremental growth levies, conversion levies, and the property tax portion of combined income tax and property tax levies be included in the 20-mill floor calculation for school funding purposes. Item 55, pages 41 to 42, on county budget commissions: This item gives county budget commissions the authority to unilaterally reduce a levy passed by voters for a school district or other taxing authority under certain circumstances. This breaches the approval of such millage by the voters. [31]
- July 21, 2025 **The House voted to override the veto of Item 66.** [32]
- Oct. 1, 2025 **The Senate completed the override,** 21 to 11, the first veto override of the 136th General Assembly. [33][34] The provision took effect about 90 days later. [34] Its effect, by the House release and Dinsmore’s summary: no new emergency or substitute levies; existing ones cannot be renewed or replaced after January 2026; no new current-expense levy where a district’s prior-year carryover exceeds 100 percent of its expenses. [32][35] Items 65 and 55 remained vetoed. [34]
- Dec. 19, 2025 **The governor signed four property tax bills:** H.B. 335, an inflation cap on inside millage; H.B. 186, an “Inflation Cap Credit” on the 20-mill floor, about $1.7 billion over three years by the House majority’s count; H.B. 129, a revised 20-mill floor formula that counts emergency and substitute levies; and H.B. 309, authority for county budget commissions to reduce millage. [36][37][38] Lebanon’s plan attributes a $13 million reduction over four years to the state’s property tax changes; Southwest’s sheet names H.B. 186, 129 and 309 as a possible further $3 million to $6 million loss. [25][20]

What happens next

### The canvass runs November 14 to 24. No margin is close enough for an automatic recount. Five-year forecasts are due November 30.

- Nov. 14 to 24 **The official canvass.** Ohio law has each county board begin its canvass between the eleventh and fifteenth day after the election and finish by the twenty-first; for November 3 that is November 14 to 24. Provisional and late-arriving absentee ballots are counted then, and the numbers above can move. [46]
- Recounts **An automatic recount** of a question or issue happens when the margin is one-half of one percent of the votes cast or less. [47] The closest results here are far outside that band: Bethel-Tate, 122 votes of 3,540 yes and no votes, 3.4 percent; North College Hill, 175 of 3,701, 4.7 percent; Lakota, 1,461 of 50,537, 2.9 percent. [4][2][1]
- Nov. 30 **Five-year forecasts.** Every district files its updated five-year forecast with the Department of Education and Workforce by November 30; the eight districts that lost will file forecasts without the revenue they asked for. [48] Fairfield’s Tier 2 reductions take effect in 2027-28 if no new revenue arrives by the end of 2026-27; North College Hill’s $1.8 million is for 2027-28. [16][18]
- Jan. 2027 **Collection begins** for Cincinnati Public’s 7 mills. Lakota’s bond collections begin in 2029, when the current bond ends. [11][13]
- Next ballots **The next elections a district could use** are the primary on May 4, 2027, and the general election on November 2, 2027, the first Tuesday after the first Monday in each month under Ohio law. [49] Five of the eleven had already returned to the ballot after a defeat: Fairfield, North College Hill and Southwest after May, Franklin for the fourth time since May 2025, and Lakota after its November 2025 bond. [27][24][15]

The editor’s take

Opinion. What follows is the editor’s view, apart from the reported record above.

I hate these levies and bonds, where we have to argue with one another in our local communities over how and whether we are going to fund our schools. Our schools need investment and support, and putting that on the backs of people like this is not great for our students or for the school experience.

We need to solve school funding at the federal level. The current administration has gutted the Department of Education. We need our federal government to better resource our local schools by channeling dollars to them. When we talk about taxes on the ultra wealthy, that is the money to harvest and put back into investing in our future and our social structures.

Dustin Nimmo, editor · Nov. 4, 2026
