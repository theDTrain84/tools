Ohio, the 2026 election

$423 million in ads on one Ohio Senate race.

Ohio's U.S. Senate race had drawn $423 million in political advertising by October 15, aired and booked ahead, by the ad tracker AdImpact's count. [1][2] As of October 12, more than $80 million of that advertising was reserved and not yet aired, for the weeks before Election Day on November 3. [2] About $350 million of the $423 million came from groups other than the two candidates' campaigns. [1] In the race for governor, advertisers on the Republican side had aired or reserved $114.6 million by October 8, and those on the Democratic side $39.9 million. [2]

The totals

Senate ads in Ohio: $106 million in 2022, $483.4 million in 2024, $423 million by October 15, 2026.

AdImpact counts the advertising in a race across broadcast, cable, satellite, radio, streaming and digital, including time booked ahead. Its 2026 count includes the candidates' own ads; $350M of the $423M came from outside groups. In 2024 the outside share was about 85 percent. [3] Figures are in the dollars of each year.

Two ways of counting the same race

AdImpact estimates the value of the airtime and digital space bought, including reservations. The Federal Election Commission counts what each committee reports it spent. Through October 23, 2026, 67 outside committees had reported $349.3 million in the Senate general election to the FEC, and the two campaigns had spent $79.6 million through October 14. [10][11][12] Ads from 501(c)(4) nonprofits that do not expressly call for a vote are reported to the FEC only in part, or not at all. Both counts are correct for what they count.

AdImpact projects $749 million in political advertising across all Ohio races this cycle. [13]

AI-made ads in Ohio and the country

April 2026: a Republican-aligned super PAC released a spot showing an AI-generated Sherrod Brown at the end of a birthday party, ending "Party's over, Sherrod." The Washington Post and Al Jazeera, which described it, did not name the committee that paid for it. [76] [77]

The rules

What the disclaimer laws require, and when the donors appear.

On the ad

Ads that support or oppose a federal candidate must say who paid. On television, an ad paid for by anyone other than the candidate must say aloud that the payer "is responsible for the content of this advertising" and show a written statement at least 4 percent of the screen's height for at least 4 seconds. [83] [84]

In Ohio

Ohio's disclaimer law, in its current version since September 30, 2025: anyone who pays for political advertising for or against an Ohio candidate or issue must identify itself on the ad; when a candidate or campaign pays, the line begins "paid for by"; broadcasters and printers may not run paid political ads without it. [85] The October 20 video in the governor's race first circulated without a "paid for" tag. [16]

Behind the name on the ad

A 501(c)(4) nonprofit may give to a super PAC without naming its own donors. One Nation gave Senate Leadership Fund $132.9 million this cycle. On air, the ad names Senate Leadership Fund. [10]

When the donors appear

No Going Back PAC, formed September 1, had reported $159.6 million in spending across its races by October 14 before any receipts were on file [24]; its first two reports, e-filed October 20 (the October monthly, covering September, and the pre-general, covering October 1 to 14), list $200 million from MAGA Inc. [14] Enough Promises Ohio PAC, formed September 29, had not disclosed its contributors by October 12 [28]; its pre-general report, e-filed October 22, lists one, Conservative Americans PAC, at $2.5 million. [29]

Ohio's pre-general report, due October 22, covers activity through October 14. From October 15 through Election Day, the campaign of the candidates for governor and lieutenant governor must also file a statement within two business days whenever one contributor's gifts in that period reach $10,000. [19] Money that passes through a state committee shows that committee's donors: Ohioans For Lower Costs shows $1 million from the IBEW. [2]
