## 1. The machine: why Ohio school districts must keep asking voters

### House Bill 920 (1976) and the tax reduction factor

- VERIFIED. The tax reduction factor lives in Ohio Revised Code 319.301. Each year the Tax Commissioner determines, for each tax a district levies, the percentage by which that tax must be reduced on "carryover property" (property that was on the tax list in both the prior and current year) so that the levy raises roughly the same dollars it raised the year before. The statute says the reduction is applied to the sums levied, not to the rate: "No reduction shall be made under this section in the rate at which any tax is levied." The reductions do not apply to fixed-sum levies (including 5705.199 substitute levies and 5748.09 combined levies), debt charges, inside millage within the one percent constitutional limitation, or municipal charter taxes. Version opened: effective Sept. 23, 2026. https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-319.301
- VERIFIED. The Legislative Service Commission's Members Brief "Property Tax Millage Floors for School Districts" (Mackenzie Damon, Sept. 19, 2025) states the purpose in one sentence: the factor exists "with the goal of preventing property taxes from increasing as quickly as property values," and "each year when property values increase, tax collections are adjusted downward so that taxing districts receive the same amount of revenue they received in the previous year." https://lsc.ohio.gov/assets/organizations/legislative-service-commission/files/property-tax-millage-floors-for-school-districts.pdf
- REPORTED. Ideastream (May 13, 2024) explains the practical effect: a voted operating, bond or permanent improvement levy is "locked at the rate in place when the levy passed," renewing an old levy keeps it at its original dollar yield, and a district gets "essentially zero growth from reappraisal" on voted levies (Howard Fleeter, economist, quoted). Fleeter: "We vote more than any other state on school levies." https://www.ideastream.org/education/2024-05-13/tired-of-being-asked-to-approve-ohio-school-levies-blame-state-law-some-say
- VERIFIED. Wikipedia's DeRolph article dates the law to 1976 and says HB 920 "effectively froze property tax revenue for schools." Use the LSC brief for the mechanism; use this only for the year. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DeRolph_v._State

### Why rising home values do not raise a district's voted-levy revenue

- VERIFIED. LSC Table 1 (Sept. 19, 2025): voted current-expense millage is subject to the reduction factor and counts toward the 20-mill floor; voted permanent improvement millage is subject to the factor but not counted toward the floor; bond and emergency levies are not subject to the factor and not counted toward the floor; unvoted inside millage is not subject to the factor. Translation for the page: when values rise 37 percent (Butler County, 2023), the effective rate on each voted operating levy is cut so the levy still produces the dollars voters approved. The district does not get the windfall. https://lsc.ohio.gov/assets/organizations/legislative-service-commission/files/property-tax-millage-floors-for-school-districts.pdf
- REPORTED. Lakota's own example, from treasurer/CFO Adam Zink's May 29, 2024 forecast post: HB 920 "ensures that a school district cannot increase the amount it collects in property taxes year over year," and Lakota's effective millage "dropped about seven mills" after the tax year 2023 triennial update. Zink: "We have had a tremendous decrease in our effective millage." https://www.lakotaonline.com/departments/operations/communications/news/news-detail-page/~board/finance/post/lakotas-five-year-financial-forecast-key-takeaways-from-treasurercfo-adam-zink
- REPORTED. Journal-News (Dec. 10, 2024): after Butler County's 37 percent appraisal increase in 2023, Lakota's local revenue rose 3.5 percent under HB 920. https://www.journal-news.com/news/lakota-schools-finances-stable-says-treasurer-but-changes-loom/article_04d659f5-8715-5510-a6fd-e5b2e62f8d53.html

### Inside versus outside millage

- VERIFIED. Inside (unvoted) millage is the share of the constitutional 10-mill limit a district receives without a vote; it is not reduced by the tax reduction factor, so it grows with values. Outside (voted) millage is everything above that, approved by voters, and is subject to the factor (LSC, Sept. 19, 2025, Table 1 and text). The constitutional source for the 10-mill limit is Article XII, Section 2 of the Ohio Constitution (cited in the OASBO HB 335 FAQ below). https://lsc.ohio.gov/assets/organizations/legislative-service-commission/files/property-tax-millage-floors-for-school-districts.pdf

### The 20-mill floor

- VERIFIED. LSC (Sept. 19, 2025): "City, local, and exempted village school districts benefit from a 20-mill floor, which guarantees that they receive at least 20 mills of property tax revenue for current expenses." Once the factor would push a district's current-expense effective rate below 20 mills, the rates are "re-adjusted so that the district's net revenue equals 20 mills worth of those taxes." Each district has two floors, one for Class I (residential and agricultural) and one for Class II. "In 2024, 477 (about 78%) of these school districts were at the 20-mill floor" out of 611. Joint vocational districts have a 2-mill floor. Statutory home: R.C. 319.301(E); constitutional authority: Article XII, Section 2a. https://lsc.ohio.gov/assets/organizations/legislative-service-commission/files/property-tax-millage-floors-for-school-districts.pdf
- VERIFIED. R.C. 319.301(E)(2) states the floor as "Two per cent of the taxable value of the property in that class" for school districts other than JVSDs and cooperative education districts, which is 20 mills. https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-319.301
- REPORTED. The floor is why some homeowners' bills track values: at the floor, "a 10% increase in a property's value will generally result in an equivalent 10% increase in property taxes" (OSBA HB 129 testimony summarized by the search index; and Signal Ohio's "safety valve" piece). The Butler County auditor's 2023 report listed eight Butler districts at the floor that got no reduction-factor relief: Edgewood, Hamilton, Madison, Middletown, Monroe, New Miami, Ross, Talawanda. Lakota was not on that list. https://finneylawfirm.com/butler-county-issues-its-property-tax-revaluation-report/ and https://signalohio.org/safety-valve-meant-to-limit-property-tax-hikes-doesnt-help-some-homeowners/
- REPORTED. Growth of the floor over time: Paul Imhoff (Buckeye Association of School Administrators) told lawmakers 168 districts were at the floor in 2019 and 409 by 2023; LSC's 2024 count is 477. (Imhoff figures via the Ohio Capital Journal, April 24, 2025.) https://ohiocapitaljournal.com/2025/04/24/chamber-backed-tax-reform-group-urges-ohio-lawmakers-stamp-out-unvoted-increases/

### The phantom revenue problem

- VERIFIED. The LSC Members Brief on DeRolph (Mariah Parr, Aug. 12, 2024), footnote 8, gives the Supreme Court's own definition from DeRolph II (2000): three types of "phantom revenue." Type I: a district did not levy the amount the state formula presumed (then 23 mills, the "charge-off"). Type II: "property tax revenue credited to a school district when its tax base increases, but which is never actually received by the district due to ... tax-reduction factors." Type III: the gap between the yield the formula assumed and the yield actually required when an income-adjusted tax base was used. Citation: 89 Ohio St.3d at 28 to 31. The court listed "eliminating 'phantom revenue'" among seven items the General Assembly had to fix. https://lsc.ohio.gov/assets/organizations/legislative-service-commission/files/derolph-v-state-school-funding-case-10020044.pdf
- Plain words for the page: the state's formula assumes a district can raise a certain local amount from its property values; HB 920 means the district often cannot actually collect that amount. The state deducts it anyway. The difference is "phantom." The 2021 Fair School Funding Plan was built to reduce this by basing local capacity on actual data (see section 3).

### The kinds of levies

- VERIFIED. Levy types a district can use, with the statute and the post-2026 status, from the Bricker Graydon alert of Dec. 22, 2025 (law firm summary, which I treat as REPORTED for the status table) cross-checked against R.C. 5705.194 as amended by HB 129 (VERIFIED, effective March 20, 2026):
  - Operating (current expense) levy, fixed rate, R.C. 5705.21: still available, new and renewal, continuing or limited term. Subject to the reduction factor. Notices must now state the district's general fund carryover percentage.
  - Emergency levy, fixed sum, R.C. 5705.194 to 5705.197: new emergency levies are now available only to districts in fiscal caution, watch or emergency or affected by a declared disaster, for up to five years, and "A tax levied under division (B) of this section may not be renewed." Existing emergency levies approved "at an election held before January 1, 2026" may be renewed for the same or lesser sum, for up to five years (division C). https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-5705.194
  - Substitute levy, R.C. 5705.199: no new substitute levies. An existing one approved before Jan. 1, 2026 may be renewed in its final year as a fixed sum, "not increased as section 5705.199 otherwise provides" (division D of 5705.194). https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-5705.194
  - Replacement levies of all types: eliminated (HB 96 veto item 66, overridden).
  - Renewal-with-increase levies: eliminated.
  - Combined income tax plus fixed-sum property tax: eliminated, new and renewal.
  - Bond issues, R.C. 133.18: unaffected. Debt levies are outside the reduction factor and outside the floor.
  - Permanent improvement levy: still available, subject to the factor, not counted toward the floor.
  - School district income tax, R.C. Chapter 5748, traditional base or earned income base: still available, new and renewal; estates no longer taxed.
  https://www.bricker.com/insights/publications/navigating-school-district-levy-options-going-forward
- VERIFIED. Emergency and substitute levies approved before Jan. 1, 2026 are to be counted in the 20-mill floor starting with the first tax year in 2026 or later in which a county containing district territory undergoes a reappraisal or triennial update (R.C. 319.301(E)(1), as amended by HB 129). Butler County's 2026 reappraisal means that switch happens for Butler districts in tax year 2026, payable 2027. https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-319.301

## 2. DeRolph: four rulings and how it ended

All from the LSC Members Brief "DeRolph v. State School Funding Case" (Aug. 12, 2024), VERIFIED, unless noted. https://lsc.ohio.gov/assets/organizations/legislative-service-commission/files/derolph-v-state-school-funding-case-10020044.pdf

- VERIFIED. Filed 1991 in Perry County Common Pleas by a coalition of districts (the Ohio Coalition for Equity and Adequacy of School Funding). Named plaintiff Nathan DeRolph, a Sheridan High School student; 550 districts joined (Wikipedia, for the plaintiff detail). Judge Linton Lewis ruled for the plaintiffs July 1, 1994; the Fifth District reversed Aug. 30, 1995. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DeRolph_v._State
- VERIFIED. DeRolph I, March 24, 1997, 78 Ohio St.3d 193, 4 to 3. The system violated the Thorough and Efficient Clause (Ohio Const. art. VI, sec. 2: the General Assembly "shall make such provisions, by taxation, and otherwise, as ... will secure a thorough and efficient system of common schools throughout the state"). One year to build a new system. The court said it did not want a "Robin Hood approach" but that there must be "but one system." The court's April 1997 clarification (Wikipedia) said property taxes could still be used but not as the primary source.
- VERIFIED. Legislative response 1997 to 1999: HB 215, SB 102 (created the Ohio School Facilities Commission, $300 million bond), SB 55, HB 412, HB 650 and HB 770 (new funding system), HB 282. HB 697 put a one-cent sales tax before voters in May 1998; it lost 79.91 to 20.09, and a related bond issue lost 60.69 to 39.31.
- VERIFIED. DeRolph II, May 11, 2000, 89 Ohio St.3d 1. Still unconstitutional. Seven items to fix: (1) overreliance on local property taxes, (2) raising the basic aid amount immediately with no phase-in or caps, (3) facilities, (4) solvency assistance, (5) funding all state mandates, (6) eliminating phantom revenue, (7) strict statewide academic guidelines. Deadline June 15, 2001.
- VERIFIED. DeRolph III, Sept. 2001, 93 Ohio St.3d 309. Most of the new system complied; the court ordered full funding of parity aid by July 1, 2003 and a recalculated base cost. On reconsideration the court ordered mediation (93 Ohio St.3d 628).
- VERIFIED. DeRolph IV, Dec. 11, 2002, 97 Ohio St.3d 434. Vacated DeRolph III, declared DeRolph I and II "the law of the case," and stated "the current school funding system is unconstitutional."
- VERIFIED (opinion text opened via Bricker's archived slip opinion). State ex rel. State v. Lewis, 99 Ohio St.3d 97, 2003-Ohio-2476, decided May 16, 2003, Lundberg Stratton writing; Pfeifer concurred; Moyer, Cook and O'Connor concurred in judgment only; Resnick and F.E. Sweeney dissented. Holding at paragraph 34: "because it is beyond doubt that Judge Lewis and the common pleas court patently and unambiguously lack jurisdiction over any post-DeRolph IV proceedings, we now grant a peremptory writ and end any further DeRolph litigation in DeRolph v. State." Paragraph 33: "The duty now lies with the General Assembly to remedy an educational system that has been found by the majority in DeRolph IV to still be unconstitutional." The U.S. Supreme Court denied certiorari Oct. 20, 2003 (540 U.S. 966). https://www.bricker.com/assets/htmldocuments/Documents/Resources/SchoolFund/051603supctderolph.pdf
- VERIFIED. LSC's closing line: "Apparently, any challenge to the school funding system enacted by the General Assembly will be filed as a new case. To date, no new case has been filed." (As of Aug. 12, 2024.)

## 3. The Fair School Funding Plan: HB 110 (2021), HB 33 (2023), HB 96 (2025)

- VERIFIED (LSC/LBO Greenbook, Department of Education and Workforce, enacted HB 96, Sept. 2025). "The FY 2026-FY 2027 budget continues the implementation of the current school funding formula established by H.B. 110 of the 134th General Assembly." The formula computes a per-pupil "base cost" from inputs (student-to-staff ratios and staff compensation), then splits it between state and local shares using each district's property wealth and income; the minimum state share is 10 percent. https://lsc.ohio.gov/assets/legislation/136/hb96/en0/files/hb96-edu-greenbook-as-enacted-136th-general-assembly.pdf
- VERIFIED (same). The six-year phase-in, Table 1: FY2022 16.67%, FY2023 33.33%, FY2024 50%, FY2025 66.67%, FY2026 83.33%, FY2027 100%. Phased-in funding = district FY2020 funding + phase-in % x (new formula amount minus FY2020 funding). "The budget completes the phase-in of the school funding formula, known as the Fair School Funding Plan, in FY 2026 and FY 2027, while maintaining FY 2022 base cost inputs."
- VERIFIED (same). The frozen inputs: "The budget retains the use of FY 2022 data for the various statewide average salary and spending inputs that comprise a district's calculated base cost." So the formula is "fully phased in" by FY2027 against a cost-of-education snapshot from 2022.
- VERIFIED (same). The guarantees: two provisions prevent a district's aid from falling below historical amounts, temporary transitional aid (FY2020 base) and the formula transition supplement (FY2021 base, including student wellness and success funds). "The budget extends both guarantees into the FY 2026-FY 2027 biennium."
- VERIFIED (same). The amounts: "Overall state foundation aid for traditional school districts is estimated at $8.15 billion in FY 2026 and $8.26 billion in FY 2027, representing annual increases of $32.5 million (0.4%) and $109.0 million (1.3%), respectively." DEW total budget $14.88 billion FY2026 and $15.30 billion FY2027; the federal line falls from $4.35 billion (FY2024 actual) to $2.97 billion (FY2025) to $2.43 billion (FY2026). DPIA (disadvantaged pupil aid) for traditional districts falls from $649.2 million (FY2025) to $564.6 million (FY2026) and $532.8 million (FY2027), because the budget changed how disadvantaged students are counted. Community and STEM school aid: $1.33 billion FY2026, $1.40 billion FY2027 (up 4.8% and 5.0%).
- REPORTED. What HB 33 (2023) did: it continued the phase-in (years three and four, 50% and 66.67%) and first used the FY2022 inputs in FY2024 (Fleeter testimony, March 4, 2025: "The FY22 inputs first used in FY24 will continue to be used in FY26 and FY27"). HB 33 also made EdChoice Expansion universal (see section 8). https://search-prod.lis.state.oh.us/api/v2/general_assembly_136/committees/cmte_h_education_1/meetings/cmte_h_education_1_2025-03-04-0215_47/testimony/2273/fleetertestimony.pdf and https://www.wvxu.org/2025-06-27/ohio-could-spend-almost-2-5b-on-vouchers-in-next-two-year-budget
- REPORTED. The 2025 fight: the House version (Speaker Huffman, spring 2025) replaced the phase-in with temporary "bridge funding" and a $226 million increase; the Senate restored years five and six "with the guarantees" but "did not increase the base cost inputs"; the conference committee kept the Senate's structure (OSBA Senate summary; Policy Matters Ohio). https://www.ohioschoolboards.org/sites/default/files/uploads/Senate%20Budget%20FINAL%20Summary.pdf and https://policymattersohio.org/news/research-tag/fair-school-funding-plan/
- REPORTED. What "underfunded" means in numbers: Policy Matters Ohio put the gap between the enacted budget and what the formula would pay with updated inputs at $2.86 billion for FY2026 to FY2027 (updated Sept. 8, 2025), after $2.75 billion for the House version and $2.74 billion for the Senate version. The count is Policy Matters', built on LSC simulations; it is an advocacy group's figure. https://policymattersohio.org/news/research-tag/fair-school-funding-plan/
- REPORTED. State share trend cited in Senate testimony (May 2025): the state's share of the formula falling from 42.35 percent in 2024 to a projected 32.2 percent by 2027, with more districts hitting the 10 percent minimum (Fair School Funding Plan WorkGroup testimony). https://search-prod.lis.state.oh.us/api/v2/general_assembly_136/committees/cmte_s_finance_1/meetings/cmte_s_finance_1_2025-05-27-0900_486/testimony/8278/senate_finance_committee_testimony_5_27_25_final_docx.pdf
- REPORTED. Lakota's own stake: Zink's May 2024 post says Lakota's state funding "remains guaranteed at FY21 levels," with simulations showing about $8 million in guaranteed aid in FY2025 rising to $12 million by the end of the phase-in, aid that "would be lost if the legislature does not continue the guarantee." The guarantee was continued in HB 96. https://www.lakotaonline.com/departments/operations/communications/news/news-detail-page/~board/finance/post/lakotas-five-year-financial-forecast-key-takeaways-from-treasurercfo-adam-zink

## 4. The pandemic cliff: ESSER

- VERIFIED (Ohio Department of Education slide deck, Aaron Rausch, April 29, 2021, hosted by OSBA). Ohio's federal K-12 relief: ESSER I $489.2 million (CARES, March 27, 2020, availability through Sept. 30, 2022); ESSER II $1.99 billion (December 2020, through Sept. 30, 2023); ESSER III $4.47 billion (ARP, estimated, through Sept. 30, 2024); GEER I $33.8 million; Coronavirus Relief Fund $100 million; BroadbandOhio $50 million; total $7.14 billion. 90 percent of ESSER II and III went to traditional districts and community schools by Title I formula. https://www.ohioschoolboards.org/sites/default/files/uploads/Federal%20COVID%20Relief%20Funding.pdf
- REPORTED. Deadlines: obligation by Sept. 30 of each round's final year (ESSER III: Sept. 30, 2024) and liquidation 120 days later (ESSER III: Jan. 28, 2025); the U.S. Department of Education allowed liquidation extensions of up to 14 months but did not extend obligation dates (Edmentum timeline; K-12 Dive). https://edmentum.com/articles/esser-funds-timeline and https://k12dive.com/news/ESSER-spending-extensions-for-schools/699553
- VERIFIED (Greenbook). The state budget's federal line shows the cliff in state totals: $4.35 billion in FY2024 actual federal funds through DEW, $2.97 billion in FY2025, $2.43 billion appropriated for FY2026. https://lsc.ohio.gov/assets/legislation/136/hb96/en0/files/hb96-edu-greenbook-as-enacted-136th-general-assembly.pdf

### Capital versus operating, said plainly

- Issue 2 is a capital ask, a bond. Under R.C. 319.301(A) debt levies are outside the reduction factor and (per LSC Table 1) outside the 20-mill floor. The money can only pay for construction and the debt on it. It does not pay teachers. Lakota's operating money still comes from the 2013 levy, inside millage, the 20-mill floor rules, and state aid guaranteed at FY2021 levels. The replacement framing (2.23 mills expiring 2028, 2.20 mills starting 2029) is a district characterization that the county auditor's millage schedule can confirm; the opposing framing (bills would drop in 2029 if it fails) is arithmetically true for the bond line only.
- The 2026 reappraisal effect on Lakota: the county median is 19 percent (2026), on top of 37 percent (2023); Nix's pre-final estimate put Lakota-area bills in the 2 to 10 percent range because Lakota is not at the floor and its voted levies are reduced by the factor; the bond, as debt, is not reduced, so a 2.2-mill bond on a higher valuation would yield more per mill, which is why the district could size the issue at 2.20 mills.

## 8. What the people who oppose more school funding propose, on the record

### Caps on carryover balances

- Governor's working group (Sept. 30, 2025): cap carryover for all taxing districts at 100 percent of annual expenses. REPORTED. https://www.ohiotownships.org/news/governors-property-tax-working-group-votes-to-issue-recommendations
- HB 96 as passed (June 2025): 40 percent for school districts, vetoed (Item 63). HB 335 as introduced: 30 percent, with county budget commission authority to cut levies above it. REPORTED and VERIFIED (OASBO FAQ). https://cms.oasbo-ohio.org/OASBO/media/Forms-and-PDFs/HB-335-FAQ.pdf
- Cost to districts: the vetoed and introduced versions were not scored as a statewide dollar loss; the mechanism cuts levies district by district. UNCONFIRMED for a statewide figure.

### Consolidation of districts

### Vouchers and EdChoice expansion

- REPORTED (Ohio Capital Journal, Megan Henry, Oct. 20, 2025, from ODEW data). State scholarship spending, five programs: FY2020 $394.2 million; FY2021 $444.5 million; FY2022 $554.5 million; FY2023 $610.2 million; FY2024 $970.7 million; FY2025 $1.09 billion (LSC had projected $1.05 billion). FY2025 by program: EdChoice Expansion 100,930 scholarships, $492.8 million, average $4,958; traditional EdChoice 42,602, $283.1 million; Cleveland 8,345, $55.1 million; Autism 6,016, $159.9 million; Jon Peterson 8,680, $103.9 million. - REPORTED (Ideastream, June 27, 2025, from LSC data). ### Replacing property tax with sales or income tax

- VERIFIED (Lincoln Institute working paper, Jared Walczak, June 2026). Replacing the roughly $21.1 billion the amendment would eliminate for schools and municipalities would take a 13.36 percent state sales tax (14.53 percent average combined) or an 8.24 percent flat state income tax (10.29 percent combined with local), with graduated alternatives pushing the top rate above 12 percent; either shifts resources "between urban and rural counties." https://www.lincolninst.edu/publication/working-papers/replacing-irreplaceable-analyzing-revenue-alternatives-ohio-property-taxes/
- REPORTED (Ohio Office of Budget and Management memo to the governor, February 2026, as summarized by the Ohio Manufacturers' Association and the Statehouse News Bureau). Property taxes raise about $24 billion a year for local governments, about equal to state income and sales tax combined; income tax rates would need to "quadruple or more," to 11 to 15 percent, or sales tax rise to about 20 percent, to replace them. https://www.ohiomfg.com/our-communities/memo-property-tax-abolition-would-gut-local-budgets/ and https://www.statenews.org/government-politics/2026-06-03/effort-to-abolish-ohio-property-taxes-faces-looming-deadline-for-fall-ballot
- REPORTED (Ideastream, July 21, 2026). Massie offered no replacement source: "It's a spending problem." Supporters pointed to lottery profits ($1.4 billion, about 12 percent of state K-12 spending) and marijuana tax ($70 million first year) (Statehouse News Bureau, June 3, 2026). https://www.statenews.org/government-politics/2026-06-03/effort-to-abolish-ohio-property-taxes-faces-looming-deadline-for-fall-ballot
- Who would pay: the abolition math moves roughly $14 billion a year of school money (63 percent of $21.4 billion in 2024, Ideastream July 21, 2026) from property owners to buyers and earners statewide; renters, who pay property tax inside rent, would get no direct cut; the HB 335 FAQ's 1991 to 2023 series (business share of school property tax 52 percent to 32.5 percent) is the school side's argument that the shift to individuals is already underway.

## 9. The counter-record

- VERIFIED (OASBO HB 335 FAQ, June 2025). "By removing revenue tools and capping growth, districts may need to seek new levies more frequently just to maintain existing services. ## 11. The story in one breath

Ohio froze school levies in 1976 so a levy raises the same dollars forever no matter how much houses appreciate, which is why a district's income flattens while its costs rise and it comes back to the ballot every few years to stand still; the Supreme Court said four times between 1997 and 2002 that leaning this hard on local property tax was unconstitutional, then closed the case in 2003 and left the fix to the legislature; the 2021 Fair School Funding Plan was the fix, but the 2025 budget finished its six-year phase-in using 2022 cost figures and a guarantee that holds most districts at 2020 or 2021 aid levels, so state foundation aid for traditional districts rises 0.4 percent this year while voucher spending passed $1 billion; the $7 billion in federal pandemic money that quietly carried payrolls from 2020 to 2024 is gone (Troy's last $1.9 million landed in fiscal 2024 and its 30-year-old renewal failed in May 2026); and in 2025 the legislature, over the governor's veto, took away emergency, substitute and replacement levies, capped growth at the 20-mill floor and on inside millage, and left the carryover cap and county-commission cuts on the table for an override before Dec. 31, 2026, while a citizen campaign to abolish property tax entirely regroups for 2027 with no replacement named and the state budget office saying income tax would have to quadruple. Lakota is the clean case: no operating levy since 2013, a 37 percent and then 19 percent reappraisal that under HB 920 barely moved its revenue, 17,348 students and 771 over capacity, and a $223.5 million, 2.20-mill, 37-year bond on Nov. 3 that replaces a 2.23-mill bond expiring in 2028, which is a buildings question, not a teachers question, and which voters cut in half from the $506 million they refused 61 to 39 a year ago. The people who oppose more school money have put real proposals on paper: a 100 percent (or 40 or 30 percent) cap on district carryover enforced by county budget commissions, consolidation of the 611 districts, vouchers as the growth line, inflation caps on every automatic increase, and replacing property tax with sales or income tax, which the Lincoln Institute prices at a 13.36 percent sales tax or an 8.24 percent flat income tax.
