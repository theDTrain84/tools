**The four moves**

1. **Name it.** Say the thing plainly, in a few words.
2. **Watch it.** Set it outside yourself and let your eyes follow it. You are the river; it is the leaf.
3. **Let it pass.** It dissolves downstream. Notice what is left.
4. **Return.** Come back to the room, and to the water whenever you need it.

One pass takes about a minute. Practiced over time, it loosens the grip.

**Where this practice comes from**

**Tashlich.** On Rosh Hashanah, Jews walk to living water, a river or the sea, and cast crumbs or empty their pockets as a sign of sins carried away. The practice draws on Micah 7:19, and it has been recorded since at least the 15th century, in the customs of Rabbi Jacob Moelin (the Maharil) of the Rhineland.

**Nagashi-bina and tōrō nagashi.** In Japan, paper dolls are set afloat on rivers in early spring to carry misfortune away, a custom rooted in Shinto purification (harae). At the close of Obon, floating lanterns are set on the water to see off the spirits of the dead.

**Leaves on a stream.** A cognitive defusion exercise from Acceptance and Commitment Therapy, developed by Steven C. Hayes and colleagues from the 1980s and in print by 1999. You picture a stream, place each thought on a leaf as it comes, and watch it float by without arguing with it.

**Expressive writing.** James W. Pennebaker's studies, from 1986 onward, asked people to write plainly about what weighed on them for a few minutes on a few days. Across many studies since, that small practice has shown modest, measurable benefits to health and mood.

From each we took one move: name it, set it outside yourself, watch it move, honor it, let it pass, return.
